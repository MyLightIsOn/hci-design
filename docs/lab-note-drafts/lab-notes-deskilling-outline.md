# Outline: Human in the Loop, Expert Out of Practice

Working topic: AI deskilling and the ironies of automation. Target ~2,000 words, Lab Notes register, technically literate non-academic audience.

## Why this topic

It extends the studio's automation-literature throughline (Lab Notes 001 built on Parasuraman & Riley; 002 ended on the claim that human expertise is "not optional" for a fifth of a structured audit). Deskilling is the missing piece: every human-in-the-loop architecture assumes the human's expertise is durable, and the research says it is not.

## Key debates in the literature

1. **Offloading vs. erosion.** Cognitive science treats offloading as an old, mostly adaptive human strategy (Risko & Gilbert, 2016; Sparrow et al., 2011). The GenAI-era work (Lee et al., 2025; Kosmyna et al., 2025 preprint) reads the same behavior as skill and engagement loss. The camps agree on more than they admit.
2. **Whether the shifted work counts as skill.** GenAI moves users from producing to verifying and "stewarding" output (Lee et al., 2025; Simkute et al., 2025). Open question: does verification exercise the same expertise that production built? No one has measured this.
3. **Whose problem it is.** Public framing blames users (overreliance) or the technology. The Human Factors tradition locates it in design (Bainbridge, 1983; Simkute et al., 2025).

## Consensus points

- Unused skills decay. Not disputed anywhere in the literature, from process control (Bainbridge, 1983) to aviation (Casner et al., 2014) to medicine (Budzyń et al., 2025).
- Cognitive work is redistributed, not eliminated (Lee et al., 2025; Tankelevitch et al., 2024).
- Engagement moderates decay. Pilots who kept their minds on task performed better (Casner et al., 2014); workers with higher self-confidence thought more critically (Lee et al., 2025).
- People are poor judges of their own offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016).

## The gap / original angle

Deskilling is discussed as a property of use. The evidence says it is a property of design: what an interface asks the user to practice determines what the user keeps. And the sharpest consequence is architectural. Human-in-the-loop review assumes the human stays expert, but daily use of the tool is what erodes the expertise the loop depends on. This connects directly to the TT Auditor finding (the remaining fifth of the audit).

## Structure (word budget ~2,000)

1. **Three months to measurable decline** (~300). The Lancet colonoscopy result as the cold open. ADR 28.4% to 22.4% unassisted after AI exposure.
2. **The 1983 warning** (~350). Bainbridge's ironies. Casner: hand-eye skills survive automation, thinking skills do not. One pull quote from Bainbridge.
3. **Offloading or erosion** (~500). The debate, the GenAI evidence, honest handling of the Kosmyna preprint and its criticism. End on the real open question: is stewardship a skill?
4. **Deskilling is a design outcome** (~450). Every interface is a training regime. What design controls: production vs. verification, AI-first vs. AI-second, confidence display, unassisted reps. Bainbridge's remedies were design remedies.
5. **The part the loop forgot** (~250). The TT Auditor tie-in. The loop consumes its own premise.
6. **The honest conclusion** (~200). Evidence limits, what is unknown, what to do meanwhile.
